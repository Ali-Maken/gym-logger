01 / Start here

Six rules that carry the whole month

You already learned rule one the hard way. Your first session was every machine in the building, and it locked your arms at 90° for three days. That was a dose problem, not a weakness problem. Nothing below will do that to you.

Leave reps in the tank

Every set should end with about 3 reps still available. Never grind to total failure this month.

Same workout, repeated

Repetition is how you learn the movements and how you measure progress. Variety is for later.

Write everything down

Weight and reps, every set. Next week you beat the number. That is the entire method.

Three days a week

Non-consecutive. Mon / Wed / Fri, or Sat / Mon / Wed. Rest days are when the work becomes muscle.

Control the weight down

Two seconds lowering, one second lifting. Slow lowering is where most of the growth comes from.

Consistency beats intensity

Three sessions a week for six months beats six a week for three weeks. Every time.

You are starting a new physical routine at a bodyweight that makes a baseline check worthwhile. If you haven't had blood pressure, blood sugar and a general check-up recently, it's a sensible thing to book in the next few weeks. Nothing here requires it — it's just useful to know your starting numbers.

02 / Vocabulary

What people are saying

You'll hear all of these in your first month. Tap any of them.

Rep & set
"three by twelve" · 3 × 12

A rep is one complete movement — push out, come back. A set is a group of reps done back to back. 3 × 12 means three sets of twelve reps, with rest between each.

Rest

The pause between sets. For this plan: 90 seconds. Long enough to recover, short enough that you finish in 45 minutes. Use your phone timer — guessing always turns into five minutes of scrolling.

Form

Whether you're doing the movement correctly. Good form means the target muscle is doing the work, the motion is smooth, and nothing is jerking or swinging. If you have to throw your body to move the weight, the weight is too heavy.

DOMS
delayed onset muscle soreness

Soreness that peaks 24–48 hours after training, not during. This is what wrecked you last week. Normal DOMS is mild stiffness that fades in two days. Arms that won't straighten is far past that, and it is not a goal to chase.

Progressive overload

The one principle everything rests on: do slightly more than last time. One more rep, or 2.5 kg more weight. Muscles only change when you keep asking for slightly more. This is also why you log everything — without a record you can't be progressive about anything.

Compound vs isolation

Compound movements use several joints and lots of muscle at once — leg press, chest press, lat pulldown, row. These are the ones that matter and they go first in your session.

Isolation movements work one muscle at one joint — bicep curl, triceps pushdown, lateral raise. These go last and get less volume. Your friend had you doing mostly isolation work, which is backwards.

Warm-up set

A light set before your working sets — roughly half the weight, 10 easy reps. Do one before your first exercise of each muscle group. It isn't training, it's rehearsal, and it costs 60 seconds.

Failure & "reps in reserve"
RIR

Failure is when you physically cannot complete another rep. Reps in reserve is how many you had left when you stopped. You want RIR 3 this month: stop when three more were possible. Beginners get almost all the benefit at RIR 3 with a fraction of the wreckage.

The stack & the pin

On a machine, the stack is the column of numbered plates. The pin is the metal peg you push into the hole to select weight. The numbers are usually kg but on some machines they're arbitrary "levels" — which is fine, since you only ever compare a machine to itself.

Superset & drop set

A superset is two exercises done back to back without rest. A drop set is finishing a set, immediately reducing the weight, and continuing. Both are intensity techniques. You don't need either this month. Mentioned only so you know what someone means.

Spotter

A person who stands over you on free weights ready to catch the bar. Since you're training alone, this is the entire reason your plan is machines: a machine can't pin you.

Re-rack

Putting weights back where you found them. Non-negotiable and the fastest way to be treated like a regular rather than a tourist.

03 / Equipment

Using a machine safely, alone

Every machine has a picture or diagram on the frame showing the movement and the seat position. Read it before you sit. Then, in order:

  • Set the seat first. The handles should line up with the joint doing the work — chest height for chest press, shoulder height for shoulder press, thighs snug under the pad for pulldown. Almost every machine adjusts with a pop-pin or a lever under the seat.
  • Write the seat number down with the exercise. Next session you sit straight down at the right height instead of experimenting.
  • Do one rep at very light weight to check the path feels natural. If a joint pinches or clicks painfully, the seat is wrong or the machine doesn't fit you. Change one, or skip it.
  • Full range, no lockout slam. Move through the whole motion, but don't bang your elbows or knees straight at the end.
  • Breathe out on the effort, in on the return. Never hold your breath through a set.

Asking gym staff to set up a machine for you is completely routine — it's what they're there for. One 10-minute walkthrough with a trainer in week 1 is worth more than any video.

04 / Week 1

One workout, three times

Same session Monday, Wednesday, Friday. Two sets each — deliberately light. Week 1 is for learning where the machines are and how they feel, not for a challenge.

Two workouts, alternating

Off days

One 30-minute brisk walk on a non-gym day. That's it. Walking is the highest-value thing you can add at your stage, and it costs you nothing in recovery.

06 / Loading

Picking the weight, and when to add

Choosing it the first time

Pick a weight you believe you could do 15 reps with — then stop at 12. If 12 felt hard, it was too heavy. If you finished and thought "I could have done ten more," add weight next set. Two or three sessions of guessing and you'll know every machine's number.

When to go up

The rule: when you complete all sets at the top of the rep range with 3 reps still in reserve, add weight next session. On machines that's usually one pin position, or 2.5 kg on dumbbells.

Expect to add weight almost every session for the first month, then much more slowly. That early jump is your nervous system learning the movement, not muscle — it's real progress but it doesn't last at that rate, and that's normal, not a plateau.

Warm-up sets

Before your first leg exercise and your first upper-body exercise, do one set of 10 at about half your working weight. Don't count it in your logged sets.

07 / Logging

The only habit that matters

One note in your phone, one entry per session. Copy this shape:

2026-08-18 · Workout A Leg press seat 4 45kg 12 / 12 / 12 easy, +weight next Chest press seat 3 25kg 12 / 11 / 10 Lat pulldown pad 5 32kg 12 / 12 / 10 Shoulder press seat 2 15kg 10 / 10 / 8 felt shaky Tri pushdown 15kg 12 / 12 Plank 30 / 30 / 25s Walk incline 6 15 min Notes: knee fine. gym busy at 7pm, go 8.

Three things make it work: the seat number so setup is instant, reps per set separately (not an average) so you can see fatigue, and a one-line note so you remember what to change.

Before each exercise, look at last session's line and beat it by one rep or one pin. That single act is the whole training method.

08 / Practical

Etiquette & what to bring

Bring

  • A small towel — put it on the bench and wipe the machine after.
  • A 1-litre water bottle. Sip through the session.
  • Flat shoes. Running shoes with soft heels are unstable under leg press; trainers with a flat sole are better.
  • Phone for timer and log. Nothing else.

Unwritten rules

  • Don't sit on a machine to rest between sets if someone's waiting — stand up beside it.
  • Ask "how many sets left?" to share. Working in alternately is normal and nobody minds.
  • Don't stand directly in front of the dumbbell rack. Step back so others can reach.
  • Wipe down what you sweat on. Every time.
  • Nobody is watching you. Everyone is looking at their own set or their own phone. This is the single most reliable fact about gyms.

Timing

6–9pm is peak everywhere, and waiting for machines will stretch a 45-minute session past 75. If your schedule allows, mornings or after 9pm mean you walk straight onto everything.

09 / Avoid

What derails beginners

Doing too much on day one

Already survived. The urge returns whenever motivation spikes — resist it.

Chasing soreness

Soreness measures novelty, not effectiveness. Trained lifters get sore rarely and progress fastest.

Changing the plan weekly

New exercises every session means you never see a number improve. Boredom is a feature here.

Skipping legs

Legs are the largest muscle mass you own and the biggest driver of results. They're also the first thing beginners drop.

Ego on the pin

Adding weight faster than form allows converts a training plan into an injury plan.

Weighing yourself daily and panicking

Weight swings 1–2 kg on water alone. Weigh weekly, same morning, same conditions, or not at all.

Training through joint pain

Muscle burn is fine. Sharp pain in a knee, shoulder or lower back is a stop signal, not a challenge.

Supplements before basics

Sleep, protein at meals, and showing up three times a week outrank every powder sold at the front desk.

10 / Safety

When to stop and get checked

Same-day medical attention

Chest pain or tightness, pain spreading to jaw or left arm, severe shortness of breath out of proportion to the effort, fainting or near-fainting, or an irregular pounding heartbeat that doesn't settle with rest.

Also: dark brown or cola-coloured urine after a heavy session, especially alongside extreme soreness and swelling. Rare, but it's the one that follows exactly the kind of session you did on day one. Worth knowing, not worth worrying about on this plan.

Stop the set, see a doctor if it persists

Sharp or stabbing joint pain, a pop or tearing sensation, numbness or tingling down an arm or leg, a lower-back pain that changes how you walk, or dizziness that returns each session.

Normal and fine: muscle burning during a set, breathlessness that settles within a couple of minutes, mild stiffness for a day or two, feeling shaky after leg press the first few times.

11 / Timeline

What actually happens, and when

  • Week 1Everything is awkward. You'll spend as long finding machines as using them. Mild soreness for a day. Nothing visible changes and nothing should.
  • Weeks 2–4Weights climb quickly — often every session. This is coordination, not muscle, and it feels great. Sessions start finishing on time. The gym stops feeling like someone else's building.
  • Month 2Strength gains slow to something steadier. Stairs and walking get noticeably easier. Clothes may fit differently before the scale moves much.
  • Month 3–6Visible change, if you've kept showing up and eating reasonably. This is also where most people quit, which is precisely why most people never get here.

On food

Training builds the strength; what you eat drives most of what the scale does. Two things need no plan and no counting: get a protein source into every meal, and stop drinking calories — sugary drinks, juice and milky tea with sugar add up faster than any session burns. Beyond that, a proper eating plan is a separate conversation worth having on its own rather than bolting onto your first month.
